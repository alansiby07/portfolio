1-Axis Spacecraft Attitude Control: PD Design with Reaction Wheel Saturation

A Python-based simulation of a single-axis spacecraft attitude control system using a reaction wheel and PD controller, optimized via grid search and verified with root locus and time-domain analysis. Published on engrXiv.

Overview

During a research internship at the Institute for Computing in Research, I designed and implemented a Python-based simulation of a single-axis spacecraft attitude control system. The simulation models reaction wheel dynamics, actuator saturation, and a proportional-derivative (PD) controller, capturing the same fundamental physics used in real spacecraft pointing systems for imaging, communications, and orbital maneuvers.

What the Simulation Does

The spacecraft is modeled as a rigid cube rotating about one axis, driven by a reaction wheel that exchanges angular momentum with the spacecraft body. A PD controller applies torque proportional to angular position error and angular velocity, with realistic constraints including maximum wheel speed, maximum motor torque, and torque saturation. The system is integrated forward in time using a fourth-order Runge-Kutta method for high numerical accuracy.

Gain Optimization

I built a grid-search optimization routine to tune the controller's proportional and derivative gains against three competing objectives: settling time, overshoot, and control torque required. This meant balancing a classic engineering trade-off: faster, more aggressive correction requires more control effort, while gentler gains take longer to settle. The optimization was tested across six spacecraft configurations, three representing large spacecraft and three representing small spacecraft, each with different moments of inertia and actuator limits.

Debugging a Flawed Performance Metric

One of the most valuable parts of the project was diagnosing a subtle bug in the settling-time detection logic. The original code considered the system "settled" the moment it re-entered an acceptable tolerance band after being outside of it without verifying it actually stayed there. This produced false-positive results that looked numerically clean but didn't reflect real system behavior. Rather than trusting output that appeared correct, I traced the logic against expected physical behavior and rewrote the detection to require the system to remain within tolerance continuously through the rest of the simulation.

Results

Across test cases, optimized gains consistently produced faster settling times and better steady-state accuracy than baseline gains, while root locus analysis confirmed the optimized pole placements sat farther into the stable left half-plane. In the small spacecraft case, settling time improved by over 40% compared to baseline. The trade-off was clear: optimized systems reached their target attitude faster but required significantly higher peak control torque; this is a real design decision every attitude control system has to make.

Publication

I co-authored the findings with my mentor and published the work on engrXiv, contributing to open-source aerospace controls research. The paper documents the full methodology, including the state-space formulation, gain estimation from design specifications, and the debugging process behind the simulation's most difficult issues.

What I Learned

Control systems theory relies heavily on linear algebra and complex analysis; subjects well beyond my coursework at the time. Most of this project was self-directed learning: working through state-space representations, understanding the difference between PD, PID, and other controller types, and learning to read root locus plots to judge system stability. I also learned a lesson that applies far beyond this project. When results look numerically clean, that's exactly when you should be most skeptical, not least. The biggest bugs in this simulation didn't throw errors; they just quietly produced wrong answers that looked right.
